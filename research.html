The central theme of my research is to explore the interplay of topology, interactions and periodic driving in quantum many-body systems. Topological phases are special in the sense that they cannot be described by local observables/order parameters in the conventional Ginzburg-Landau theory. Instead, they are characterized by global geometric properties of the gapped many-body ground state, which are robust against symmetry-preserving perturbations that do not close the energy gap. Thus, a topologically non-trivial state cannot be adiabatically connected to a trivial state (a tensor product of single-particle states) without crossing a quantum phase transition.

Higher-order topological insulators

"First-order" topological insulators are characterized by the presence of robust, gapless states on their boundaries with codimension one. In contrast, "higher-order" topological insulators (HOTIs) host robust, gapless states on their boundaries with codimension two or higher. For example, a 2D second-order topological insulator hosts gapless, bound states at its 0D corners, while a 3D second-order topological insulator hosts gapless, bound states at its 1D hinges. HOTIs usually require the presence of certain crystalline symmetries for their protection, and thus extend the notion of topological crystalline insulators.

In particular, the topological classification of HOTIs with anti-commuting crystalline mirror symmetries is still an active area of research. This is due to the complex relationship between edge topology, Wilson loop operators and crystalline symmetries. In our work, we have developed momentum-space topological invariants from Wilson loops and "periodicized" Wilson lines to characterize the topology of the Wilson loop spectra of HOTIs with anti-commuting mirror symmetries. We also study effects of mirror-symmetry-breaking perturbations on edge gap closings and find novel "edge-selective" topological phase transitions, resulting in bound states appearing at a subset of boundaries.

Publications:

Periodically-driven (Floquet) systems

Periodically driving a quantum system out of equilibrium, also known as Floquet engineering, can lead to the emergence of novel phases of matter that are not possible in static systems. Specifically in graphene, Floquet engineering using circularly-polarized lasers can open a topologically non-trivial gap at the Dirac points, due to broken time-reversal symmetry, resulting in a Floquet Chern insulator. Recent experiments have even measured the quantized Hall conductance (light-induced anomalous quantum Hall effect) and observed Floquet-Bloch states in graphene.

An interesting scenario arises when the vector potential amplitude of the driving field has a radial vortex profile with a phase twist, due to a non-zero orbital angular momentum (OAM) of light, about the vortex core.
